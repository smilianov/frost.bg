How to read this

The page gives you two pairs of dates for your point, and the history behind them. Here is what each number means.

The two pairs of dates

For any point, the page gives four dates: typical and safe for spring, typical and safe for autumn. The two seasons are computed separately — each from its own years with data, because a given cell can be missing spring dates only, or autumn dates only.

The typical date is the median of the years with data for that season — close to "half the years," but not exactly: for Manole, 16 of the 30 years had the last spring frost on or before the typical date, and 16 of the 30 had the first autumn frost on or before the typical date too. With an even number of years, the choice is conservative: the later of the two middle dates in spring, the earlier one in autumn.

The safe date is a percentile of the same years — the 90th in spring, the 10th in autumn. The two boundaries are separate statistics for separate seasons, not one combined promise: for Manole, 3 of the 30 years had frost after the safe spring date (April 11), 2 of the 30 had frost before the safe autumn date (October 30), and 25 of the 30 had neither.

For Manole, over 30 years of data: typical March 29 / November 25, safe April 11 / October 30.

Tip: in your first year at a new place, use the safe date — sowing a week later costs less than a frozen seedling.

The chart

Each point on the chart is one year: a circle for the last spring frost, a diamond for the first autumn frost. The axis on the left is the months.

Arrow keys (or a finger on a phone) pick a year and show its dates.

Points outside the selected period are hollow, not hidden. The chart's range doesn't change with the period, so years never appear to vanish.

The 10, 20 and 30-year periods

The dates are recalculated for the selected period from the same data, with no new request to the server.

There is no period shorter than 10 years: the safe date only makes sense with at least 10 years with frost for that season — a smaller sample isn't one you can trust.

Below the dates is how many days the typical frost has shifted over the last 10 years compared with all 30 — so you can see whether frost is coming earlier or later at your place than before.

The risk after a date

Example for Manole: in 1 of 30 years there was frost after April 20 and before July 1 — 3%.

This is a historical frequency for this cell and this date, not a forecast for this year; frost on the date itself does not count — only after it.

It answers for spring only: the first autumn frost before a given date says nothing about the days that follow.

The safe date works on the same idea, just with a fixed target instead of a date you pick — for your own point, use the "Risk of frost after a date" control on the results page.

The frost-free season

For each of the 30 years separately, the frost-free season length is computed — the days between that year's last spring frost and first autumn frost, not counting either frost day.

The typical length is the median of those 30 yearly values — with an even number of years, the median is the average of the two middle ones: for Manole, (236 + 238) / 2 = 237 days. The shortest (194 days, 1997) and longest (281 days, 2023) are real, individual years, not a calculation.

That's why subtracting the two typical dates gives a different number: the same calculation, applied directly to March 29 and November 25, gives 240 days, while the median of the yearly lengths is 237. The median of the differences is not the difference of the medians.

The two elevations

The grid cell is about 9 km across and has its own average elevation. For Manole the cell is 42.2, 24.9, at an elevation of 99 m.

The point you pick or search for is usually elsewhere in that cell and can be at a different elevation — for Manole the point is ≈ 152 m.

The two come from different models and do not correct the dates — they're there for you to judge for yourself.

ERA5 is a 9–25 km grid; in valley bottoms the night minimum is usually overestimated, meaning frost is underestimated: real dates there may be later in spring and earlier in autumn than shown. This is a tendency, not a rule — the elevation difference alone isn't enough to tell you whether or by how much.

Where the numbers come from

The data is ERA5-Land via Copernicus CDS, 30 full years. Frost means a day with a 2 m minimum temperature ≤ 0°C.

The grid has 0.1° cells (≈ 9 km) and was precomputed for 2,080 points over Bulgaria.

A year with under 300 valid days doesn't count for that cell. The 10-year threshold applies separately to spring and autumn, and separately to the selected period — so a cell can have spring dates but no autumn dates, or the other way round.

A table row with no data for that year is labeled "not enough data". A date that wasn't recorded in a year that does have data is labeled "no frost recorded" — those are not the same thing.

Why it doesn't match what I see

The grid is a model, not a thermometer in your garden: one cell is about 9 km across (≈ 11 km north–south, ≈ 8 km east–west), which is close to 90 km² under one and the same number — with a single average elevation for that whole area, no microrelief and no cold pocket behind the fence.

Use the dates as a frame for planning, not a verdict — and keep your own notes for your place. Over time, they become your best guide to your own garden specifically.
