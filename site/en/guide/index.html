How to read this

The page gives you two pairs of dates for your point, and the history behind them. Here is what each number means.

The two pairs of dates

For any point, the page gives four dates: typical spring and typical autumn frost, and safe spring and safe autumn.

The typical date is the median of all the years: in half of them the frost is already over by that date, and in the other half it isn't.

The safe date is more cautious — the 90th percentile in spring and the 10th in autumn: in 9 years out of 10 there is no frost after the spring safe date, and none before the autumn one.

For Manole, over 30 years of data: typical March 29 / November 25, safe April 11 / October 30.

Tip: in your first year at a new place, use the safe date — sowing a week later costs less than a frozen seedling.

The chart

Each point on the chart is one year: a circle for the last spring frost, a diamond for the first autumn frost. The axis on the left is the months.

Arrow keys (or a finger on a phone) pick a year and show its dates.

Points outside the selected period are hollow, not hidden. The chart's range doesn't change with the period, so years never appear to vanish.

The 10, 20 and 30-year periods

The dates are recalculated for the selected period from the same data, with no new request to the server.

There is no period shorter than 10 years: under 10 years with frost, a "safe in 9 years out of 10" date is meaningless — the sample is too small.

Below the dates is how many days the typical frost has shifted over the last 10 years compared with all 30 — so you can see whether frost is coming earlier or later at your place than before.

The risk after a date

Example: "in 4 of 30 years there was frost after April 20 and before July 1 — 13%".

This is a historical frequency, not a forecast for this year; frost on the date itself does not count — only after it.

It answers for spring only: the first autumn frost before a given date says nothing about the days that follow.

The same sentence explains the safe date too — by construction, it's the date with about a 10% risk.

The frost-free season

Typical, shortest and longest length of the frost-free season — the days between the last spring frost and the first autumn frost.

Each of the three is computed per year first, and only then averaged.

That's why subtracting the two typical dates gives a different number: for Manole, 240 days, while the true typical season length is 237.

The two elevations

The grid cell is about 9 km across and has its own average elevation. For Manole the cell is at 42.2°N, 24.9°E, 99 m.

The point you pick or search for is usually elsewhere in that cell and can be at a different elevation — for Manole the point is ≈ 152 m.

The two come from different models and do not correct the dates — they're there for you to judge for yourself.

ERA5 overestimates the night minimum in valley bottoms, meaning it underestimates frost: if you're lower than the cell, expect a later spring frost and an earlier autumn frost than shown.

Where the numbers come from

The data is ERA5-Land via Copernicus CDS, 30 full years. Frost means a day with a 2 m minimum temperature ≤ 0°C.

The grid has 0.1° cells (≈ 9 km) and was precomputed for 2,080 points over Bulgaria.

A year with under 300 valid days doesn't count for that cell. A cell with under 10 years with frost has no dates.

An empty year in the table means "not enough data". An empty date within a year means "no frost was recorded" — those are not the same thing.

Why it doesn't match what I see

The grid is a model, not a thermometer in your garden: a 9 km cell, an average elevation, no microrelief, no cold pocket behind the fence.

Use the dates as a frame for planning, not a verdict — and keep your own notes. After three years, your own records beat any model.
